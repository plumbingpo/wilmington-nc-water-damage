Crawl Space Water Removal in Wilmington

A flooded crawl space rarely announces itself. It tends to turn up as a damp chill underfoot, a stale smell at the vents, or a small lake discovered the day somebody lifts the hatch. Usually that means days of moisture in the joists already, plus insulation that is wet all the way through. Crews from our company extract the water, haul away anything that cannot be saved and dry the structure ahead of rot.

Pumping is just half of the work. Floor joists, beams and the subfloor absorb moisture slowly and let go of it slowly, so we take moisture readings on the wood and keep dehumidifiers running until the numbers come down, usually 3 to 5 days. Finding the cause matters just as much, since a dripping water line, a failed drain or groundwater from a storm will flood the area again unless it is addressed.

Signs to Look For

  • Fuzzy patches, pale or dark, spreading across the framing or subfloor
  • Steel posts, hangers for pipes and duct straps showing orange rust
  • A first floor with a cool, moist feel and some give when you walk on it
  • An increase in insects and pests that like damp, dark spaces
  • Fiberglass insulation sagging or hanging down between the floor joists
  • A look through the hatch shows pooled water or soupy soil
The Clock

What Happens if You Wait

First day

Puddles Form Underneath

Low areas fill first while the ground underneath takes on water. Batts begin drawing moisture upward and the air below the house turns humid quickly.

24 to 48 hours

Early Mold Growth

Mold can start on damp joists and paper faced insulation. Soaked insulation gains weight and starts to sag off the floor above.

Day 3 to 7

Dampness Rises Indoors

Damp air rises into the living space. The lumber goes on taking in moisture, a stale smell spreads and flooring can begin to cup.

Past one week

Rot Sets In

Wood that stays wet begins to decay. Joists and sill plates soften, fasteners rust and the damp space attracts termites and other pests.

Our Process

What Happens After You Call

1

Safety Check and Inspection

Nobody enters until the space has been screened for shock risks, hanging ducts and contamination. Next comes charting the extent of the water and metering the joists and subfloor for moisture.

2

Pump Out Water

Submersible pumps and extraction hoses remove the standing water, including the low spots and footing trenches where it collects. Mud and silt left on the liner are cleared at the same time.

3

Remove Wet Materials

We bag up the wet insulation, damaged vapor barrier and loose debris and take it all outside. Our crew then wipes down the uncovered framing and applies antimicrobial to spots with growth or a high chance of it.

4

Dry and Verify

Compact fans and dehumidification work in the crawl space as long as it takes for the framing to test normal. We recheck with a meter and give you the readings and a prevention plan.

Behind the Surface

What Hides Behind It

Water That Is Not Clean

What collects beneath a home is frequently dirty. Groundwater full of soil and waste, or a drain leak, counts as Category 2 or 3, which calls for protective equipment and disinfecting.

Rot You Cannot See

Rot often starts on top of the joist or at the sill plate, where you cannot see it from the hatch. Our crew tests the lumber by hand to locate weak areas.

Settling Under the House

Long periods of standing water loosen the soil that holds up the support piers. Doors that stick and floors that slope upstairs are possible signs.

Wet Wiring and Ducts

Most crawl spaces carry wiring, junction boxes and HVAC ducts. Wires under water can shock someone, and wet duct wrap pushes a moldy smell through every vent.

A shop vac can handle a small puddle of clean water. It will not dry joists and subfloor, and a box fan mostly moves damp air around. Crawl spaces are also tight, dark and may have live wiring or sewage in the water. Professional pumping and drying is the safer choice once the water is more than a thin puddle.
We do not recommend it while water is standing. Electrical cables and boxes are common down there, and wet ones can energize the water. There can be sewage or bacteria from the ground in it too, plus tight quarters and stale air. Shine a light in from outside the opening if you like, and leave the rest to a trained crew.
Removing the standing water is quick, generally a matter of a few hours. The wood needs more time, most often 3 to 5 days of air movers and a dehumidifier working below the floor. Thick beams, a dirt floor with no liner, or water that sat for weeks can add time. The job ends when the wood tests dry, whatever the date says.
Mold can begin on damp wood and paper faced insulation within 24 to 48 hours. Little air moves down there and no sun reaches it, so natural drying is very slow. Removing the water and lowering the humidity quickly is the best way to limit growth. Where growth has already started, we clean the wood and treat it with an antimicrobial.
Usually it does need to go. Once soaked, batts trap water, no longer insulate well and keep the wood they touch damp. They also sag and fall once they are heavy. Our crew pulls the soaked sections to let the framing dry, then new batts are installed after the wood meters normal again.
